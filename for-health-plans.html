What 2026 state law actually says

Four statutes, four different mechanisms — and one common thread: the plan, not the vendor, answers for the AI’s outcomes.

Alabama · SB 63

Effective Oct 1, 2026

The AI certification rides the existing annual utilization-review filing, due on or before July 1 each year, attested by an “authorized representative.” The statute permits the attestation to rest on reasonable reliance on internal policies and third-party vendors. The open question: what makes that reliance reasonable if the Department of Insurance inquires.

Colorado · HB 26-1139

Effective Jan 1, 2027

AI outcomes must be “periodically reviewed to maximize accuracy and reliability,” with records “sufficient to demonstrate compliance.” A periodic review with records means someone has to run the review and keep the evidence — and be able to say who ran it.

Washington · SB 5395

Effective Jun 11, 2026

AI cannot be the sole basis for a medical-necessity denial — and plans must report the percentage of denials aided by AI. Reporting a percentage presumes the plan can measure, reliably, where AI touched the decision.

Iowa · HF 2635

Effective Jul 1, 2026

AI cannot be the sole basis for a medical-necessity denial. Demonstrating that a human judgment — not the algorithm alone — carried each denial is an evidence question, not a policy question.

None of these statutes ask whether the vendor’s dashboard is impressive. They ask what the plan can show.

The examination layer is arriving on schedule

The NAIC’s AI Risk Evaluation Supplement v5.0 is out for public comment (comments due September 29, 2026), with an adoption vote expected at the Fall National Meeting in November 2026. Once adopted, AI reviews fold into standard market-conduct and financial examinations — not a separate, special event.

Routine exams, not one-off inquiries

When AI questions live inside the standard exam, the evidence has to exist before the exam letter arrives. Assembling it afterward is the expensive way.

Accountability does not transfer to the vendor

The NAIC’s work on third-party data and models has established this plainly: buying the AI from someone else does not move the obligation to someone else. The plan answers.
